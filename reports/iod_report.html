Uganda Short Rains and the IOD
Climate brief · Part 3Indian Ocean DipoleJan 1990 – Dec 2025

The Indian Ocean, not El Niño, sets the short rains

Parts 1 and 2 found no El Niño effect on Uganda's short rains. Measured over October–December and against the Indian Ocean Dipole, a clear signal appears: a warm western Indian Ocean brings wetter short rains across most of the country, and El Niño matters mainly because it tends to trigger that pattern.

Key takeaways

  1. The IOD drives much of the short rains' year-to-year swings. Using October–December, it correlates significantly with five of seven series, most strongly in Central (r = 0.59) and the Lake Victoria basin (r = 0.54).
  2. El Niño acts through the IOD. With the IOD held fixed, El Niño's correlation with the short rains is close to zero everywhere. All six positive-IOD seasons since 1990 also had El Niño conditions.
  3. The earlier null result came from the season window. The IOD's influence peaks in November and December. A September–November window leaves out half of it.
  4. Strong events carry the signal. Without 1997 and 2019 the correlations fall by about 40%, so the IOD tells you most in strong years.
  5. The IOD also shapes the June–August rains, the main growing period in the north and northeast. With El Niño held fixed, it correlates with mid-year rain in every region, and this signal doesn't depend on a few extreme years.
  6. The IOD does not explain Karamoja's wetting trend, and neither index affects the long rains (March–May).

01 · The two indices

The IOD and El Niño usually rise together

The Dipole Mode Index measures how much warmer the western Indian Ocean is than the eastern. The Oceanic Niño Index measures El Niño in the Pacific. Both are October–December averages in °C. The shaded band marks ±0.4 °C: seasons above it count as positive IOD, below it as negative.

Why it mattersThe two indices correlate at r = 0.70, and every positive-IOD season since 1990 came with El Niño conditions. That overlap is why Parts 1–2 looked for an El Niño effect, and why Section 04 has to separate the two statistically. 2019 comes closest to an IOD-only event: a very strong IOD with only a weak El Niño.

ReadingThis matches the published view that El Niño years bring wet East African short rains mainly when they trigger a positive IOD. Because the two overlap so much in this record, the separation rests on the moderate years where they differ, so treat it as strong evidence rather than proof.
05 · Mid-year rains

In June–August the IOD and El Niño pull in opposite directions

June–August brings about 36% of Karamoja's yearly rain and a similar share in the Northern region: it is the heart of their single growing season. In these months a positive IOD brings more rain, but El Niño brings less. Because the two often occur together, the effects partly cancel, which hides the IOD signal until El Niño is held fixed.

08 · Trends

The IOD doesn't explain Karamoja's wetting

Part 2 found significant upward short-rains trends in Karamoja, Eastern and Northern. Here each trend is re-estimated with the IOD added to the model. The IOD itself has no significant trend over 1990–2025 (+0.08 °C per decade), so it can't account for a steady rise.

ReadingKaramoja's trend barely changes (Sep–Nov +3.5 → +3.4 mm/yr, still p = 0.003). The national and Northern trends lose significance, so part of their rise reflects strong IOD years late in the record (2018, 2019, 2023). The IOD explains year-to-year swings instead: adding it raises the variance explained in Central's Oct–Dec rainfall from 2% to 34%.
09 · Limits and next steps

How far to trust these numbers

Solid

  • The October–December IOD correlations in Central, the Lake Victoria basin, Eastern and nationally survive a strict false-discovery-rate correction across all 91 tests; Northern sits just above the line
  • The June–August IOD signal holds in every region once El Niño is removed, and doesn't depend on extreme years
  • The IOD's effect is concentrated in November–December in every region, with a second window in June–July
  • Neither index has any link with the long rains
  • Karamoja's trend is not an IOD artefact

Handle with care

  • The IOD and El Niño overlap heavily, so the partial correlations rest on a few informative years
  • Only six positive and three negative IOD seasons in 36 years; the composite differences are not individually robust
  • The signal depends heavily on 1997 and 2019
  • CHIRPS blends satellite and gauge data; changes in the gauge network can affect it

Recommended next steps. Test whether the September IOD value forecasts October–December rainfall out of sample (the synthesis report does this). Check Karamoja's trend against station records or a second rainfall product. Look at rainfall intensity and rainy-day counts, not only totals.
