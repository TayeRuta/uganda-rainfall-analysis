What 36 years of rain mean for Uganda's farms

A combined reading of the national, regional and ocean-driver analyses. Uganda's total rainfall is not trending, but the northeast is getting wetter, the second rainy season is both the riskiest and the most predictable, and a reading of the Indian Ocean in early September gives two to three months' warning of how the October–December rains will turn out across the south, centre and east.

Main findings

  1. National totals are stable; the change is regional. Uganda's annual rainfall shows no significant trend, but Karamoja (+6.7 mm/yr) and the Eastern region (+7.2 mm/yr) are getting wetter, mostly in September–October. Medium
  2. Bad years are mostly nationwide. Regional seasonal totals move together (average correlation 0.72–0.75), so a poor season in one region usually means a poor season in most. The northeast and the west are the least connected. High
  3. The two seasons are independent. A failed March–May season says almost nothing about October–December. Each season's risk has to be managed on its own. High
  4. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) drives the short rains. It explains much of the year-to-year swing in October–December rainfall, and El Niño's effect runs largely through it. It also influences the June–August rains in the north and east. High for Oct–Dec, Medium for Jun–Aug
  5. There is a usable early warning for the second season, but not the first. The July–August IOD, known by early September, forecasts October–December rain with modest, cross-validated skill in the centre, south and east. Nothing in this analysis forecasts the March–May rains. Medium
  6. Failed seasons are not becoming more frequent. The 2000s had the most failed seasons, centred on the 2008–09 drought. The 2010s and 2020s have had fewer, though rainfall became more erratic month to month. Medium

01 · Rain regimes

Two farming calendars: two seasons in the south, one long season in the north

Share of each region's yearly rainfall falling in each season. The south and west get about a third each from March–May and September–November, which supports two cropping seasons. Karamoja and the Northern region get more than a third of their rain in June–August, in a single long season from April to September.

ReliabilityThe two seasons are not equally dependable. Nationally, October–December totals swing by 20% from year to year, against 13% for March–May. In Karamoja the short rains swing by 39%. For most regions the second season is the riskier one.

03 · Regions together

When one region has a bad season, most do

Correlation between regions' seasonal totals. Values near 1 mean two regions' good and bad years almost always coincide. The average across all pairs is 0.75 for October–December, so poor seasons tend to be national events. The weakest links are between Karamoja and the Western region.

ImplicationBecause regional harvests rise and fall together, buying grain from another region within Uganda offers only a partial buffer in a bad year. The northeast and the southwest are the pairing most likely to differ.

06 · Early warning

An early-September reading of the Indian Ocean gives two to three months' warning

The IOD is monitored weekly, so its July–August average is known by early September, around the time second-season planting begins in the two-season south. Each region's October–December rainfall was predicted from that value, and from the September value, holding each year out in turn so it never helped predict itself (leave-one-out cross-validation). The bars show how much that cut forecast error compared with assuming an average season.

How good is it?Useful, not decisive. In Central and the Lake Victoria basin the July–August IOD cuts forecast error by about a fifth, and picks the right third (wet, normal or dry) in 56–58% of years, against one in three by chance. Waiting for September's value improves that to about a quarter, but arrives after much of the planting. After a strong positive July–August IOD, national October–December rain was above the median in 5 of 6 seasons. There is little or no skill for Karamoja or the Western region. Operational forecasts from ICPAC and the Uganda National Meteorological Authority use more information than this and should come first; this result shows why the IOD is central to them.

08 · For agriculture

What this means by farming zone

These are implications of the rainfall evidence, not tested agricultural results. Each one should be checked against crop, yield and price data before it informs a decision. The zones follow the regions used in this analysis.

CENTRAL · LAKE VICTORIA BASIN · WESTERN

Two-season south and west

  • March–May is the dependable season (13% swing nationally), but nothing here forecasts it. Drought-tolerant varieties, insurance and water storage are the main tools.
  • October–December is riskier but forecastable. A strong positive IOD in July–August points to a wet season: an opportunity for longer-maturing varieties, but also a warning for waterlogging, flooding and wet-harvest losses. A negative IOD points to a dry season.
  • The Western region is the steadiest and least tied to the IOD, so it is the least forecastable from the ocean.
EASTERN

Getting wetter, and strongly ocean-driven

  • Rainfall is rising (+7.2 mm/yr), mostly in September.
  • It has the strongest June–August IOD link and a solid October–December one, so both of its main growing periods carry an ocean signal.
  • Positive-IOD years bring the biggest surges (2019's short rains were 94% above average). Flood preparedness should follow the IOD forecast.
NORTHERN

One long season, hit hardest in 2009

  • About 35% of the year's rain falls in June–August. The 2009 drought lasted 9 months here, the longest anywhere since 1990.
  • The IOD links to its June–August rains once El Niño is held fixed, and El Niño itself tends to dry the mid-year season: a developing El Niño without a positive IOD is the risk scenario.
  • No significant long-term trend in any season.
KARAMOJA

Driest, most erratic, and changing

  • 917 mm a year with a 17.5% annual swing, twice the national figure: the highest-risk farming environment in the country.
  • The extra rain arrives in September–October, at the end of the April–September season. That could lengthen the growing window, but rain at harvest also raises drying and storage losses. Both are worth testing against crop data.
  • No usable October–December forecast skill from the IOD. The June–August signal is more promising (partial r = 0.44).
NationallyBecause regions' bad seasons coincide, food-security planning can't rely on one region offsetting another. Shocks to national production are likely to be correlated, which strengthens the case for strategic reserves, index insurance and early action triggered by seasonal forecasts.
09 · Confidence

How much weight each finding can bear

Confidence combines the strength of the statistics, whether independent tests agree, whether the result survives multiple-testing correction, and whether it fits the published climate science. High results can inform decisions now. Medium results are likely, but need confirming with a second dataset. Low results are leads for further work.

10 · Methods, limits and next steps

How this was done, and what it can't tell you

Data

CHIRPS daily rainfall (UC Santa Barbara), summed to monthly totals and averaged over six regions and the whole country in Google Earth Engine, with permanent open water masked. IOD: Dipole Mode Index (HadISST1.1, NOAA). ENSO: Oceanic Niño Index (ERSSTv5, NOAA). January 1990 to December 2025.

Statistics

Linear and Mann–Kendall trend tests; Pearson, Spearman and partial correlations; SPI-3 drought index; leave-one-out cross-validated regression for forecast skill; Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across the 91 driver tests in Part 3. Significance means p < 0.05 before correction unless stated.

Main limits

One rainfall product, with a changing gauge network. Monthly totals can't show rainy-season onset, dry spells or intensity, which often matter more to crops than totals. 36 years is short for climate attribution. The four administrative regions stand in for agro-ecological zones.

Next: from rainfall to agriculture. Link these rainfall series to crop production and yields (UBOS agricultural surveys, FAOSTAT), vegetation condition (satellite NDVI), and food prices, to measure how much each rainfall signal actually moves harvests and food security. Daily CHIRPS data would add onset dates and dry spells, the rainfall measures most closely tied to crop failure. The first step is done: rainfall shocks and food prices.
