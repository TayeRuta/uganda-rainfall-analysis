36 years of rain over Uganda: stable totals, restless seasons

Uganda gets about the same amount of rain each year as it did in 1990. What has changed is how unevenly it arrives: wetter and drier decades, more erratic months, and one drought in 2009 that lasted most of a year.

Key takeaways

  1. No reliable long-term trend. Annual rainfall rose slightly on paper, but two separate statistical tests say the change could be chance.
  2. Year-to-year swings are the real risk. The driest and wettest years differ by 509 mm, about 40% of a normal year.
  3. 2009 stands alone. Rain stayed below normal for eight straight months, twice as long as any other drought since 1990.
  4. The short rains are shifting. September–November totals are edging up and swinging more widely. The March–May long rains have held steady.
  5. Months are getting more erratic. Monthly variability rose about 12% from the 1990s to the 2010s, even though annual totals stayed flat.

03 · The rain calendar

Two rainy seasons, with April the wettest month

Uganda's year has two peaks: the long rains (March–May) and the short rains (September–November). April averages 167 mm but has ranged from 104 to 246 mm. November is the least predictable month.

04 · Two seasons

The short rains are creeping up and getting less predictable

The March–May long rains, the main planting season for much of the country, average 417 mm with no trend. The September–November short rains average 378 mm, are rising about 1.6 mm a year (borderline, p = 0.06) and vary more from year to year.

08 · El Niño and La Niña

At national scale, El Niño years don't stand out

El Niño is linked to wetter short rains across East Africa. In this national average, short-rains totals look almost the same across El Niño, neutral and La Niña years (p = 0.80). The signal is likely real in some regions and diluted by averaging the whole country.

09 · Limits and next steps

What this dataset can and can't tell us

Good for

  • Long-term trend testing for annual and seasonal totals
  • The seasonal calendar: when rain usually falls
  • Ranking historical droughts and wet spells
  • Comparing decades and tracking variability
  • Measuring how long and how severe droughts were
  • Broad tests of climate drivers such as ENSO

Not suited for

  • District or regional drought risk (Karamoja is hidden in the national average)
  • Pinning a single year's drought on one cause
  • Forecasting more than about a month ahead from rainfall alone
  • Crop-specific impact, which needs soil and planting data
  • Flood or single-storm events (this is monthly data)
  • Formal climate-change attribution (36 years is short)

Recommended next step. Re-run the same pipeline for specific regions: Karamoja, the Lake Victoria basin and Uganda's agro-ecological zones. The sharpest droughts and the clearest El Niño signal are likely being smoothed away by the national average.
